Why

Trigger.dev runs our background jobs. It rents a small computer for each job and bills every second that computer is on. Reading the news costs about $906 a month there, 83% of the whole Trigger.dev bill on 29 September.

Gemini, the AI that reads each article, costs us nothing: Google’s console shows $7.58K of September usage and $7.58K of credits against it, so the total is $0. That makes Trigger.dev seconds the thing to cut. One thing to watch: the $9,000 monthly spend cap stands at $8,231, and when it tripped on 29 September most of that week’s 2,352 failed “who is mentioned” jobs followed. It resets on 1 October.

How it works

  1. The goal. Rooms are topic pages, like the Iran war or the AI race, meant to stay current with the news. An AI reads each of about 10,000 articles a day and pulls out the useful parts.

  2. Articles come in. Scheduled jobs check news services every few minutes, and every new article is handed straight to processing.

  3. One question per step. A manager job passes the article through steps, and each asks Gemini one question: who is mentioned; then claims, events and topics; then which rooms want it; then, per room, a keep-or-reject label.

  4. The meter. Each step is its own job on its own computer. Most of its seconds are spent waiting for Gemini to answer, and the meter runs while it waits.

  5. Why the manager is nearly free. When a job waits with Trigger.dev’s own wait, Trigger.dev saves its memory and switches the computer off until the wait ends. The manager waits that way: alive for about 246 seconds, billed for under 1. A call to Gemini is an ordinary internet call, which Trigger.dev cannot pause.

1. What comes in

  1. B1 conspectus-3eu8j.2 · Read only the first two copies of each story PR #2842

    What it is. When OpenAI launches a model and 30 sites write it up, the news service groups the 30 write-ups as one story, and we read only the first two instead of all 30. Two, not one, because the first copy is sometimes a thin rewrite no room keeps (decided 30 Sep).

    How we know they are the same story. The news service does the grouping itself and sends a story ID with each article; we run no AI or comparison of our own. It only groups articles it has matched, so 56% of the AI race articles arrive with no story ID and would always be read.

    What reading every copy gives us. Mostly repeats: 30 write-ups of one launch produce 30 near-identical sets of claims and topics, and the events step pays to find the same event 30 times before its duplicate check merges them.

    Why we need it. 15–17% of everything we read is a repeat write-up of a story we already read.

    Without it. We keep paying to read the same story many times over.

    Done when. No story has more than two write-ups read, about 15% fewer articles go through the AI, and rooms lose no more than 2.4% of the stories they keep.

  2. B2 conspectus-3eu8j.3 · Block news sites that never produce anything we keep PR #2835 In scope

    What it is. A setting on the AI race news search that ignores sites which sent 5 or more articles last week with none kept.

    Why we need it. Those sites are 5% of what we read and almost never useful.

    Without it. We keep paying to read sites no room ever keeps.

    Done when. The following week has nothing from the blocked sites, about 5% fewer articles, and no more than 2% fewer kept stories.

Long’s four questions

1. Does asking Gemini four things at once cost more than one after another?

No. The same questions use the same words, so the Gemini bill is the same; only the Trigger.dev seconds change, and they go down. Three things do change:

  • More requests land in the same minute, so we reach Google’s per-minute limits for our Tier 3 account sooner. A limit hit means a wait and a retry, not a charge. We haven’t looked up those limits yet.
  • The database takes more at once. The events step is already held to 15 at a time because 85 at once slowed the shared database.
  • If one of the four questions fails, the job has to retry only that one, or it re-asks the other three.

2. Midterm markets

Descoped. What we found is kept under “For agents”.

3. Is “$500–750 a month” a saving?

Yes: an estimated saving on the Trigger.dev bill, out of about $780 a month that the four AI steps cost today. It comes from putting several articles in one job and asking all their questions at the same time, so we pay for the slowest wait instead of every wait added up. It is our arithmetic, not a measurement; a trial on one step would confirm it.

4. Is “5% fewer jobs” worth doing?

On its own it is small, about $40 a month. It is still worth doing because it is a setting on the feed with no code, and it adds to keeping two copies per story: together 19% fewer jobs, about $150 a month, for 4% of good stories. It saves nothing on the news service, because our searches are prepaid.

Same work, different shape

Yes: the biggest saving keeps every news search, every article and every Gemini question, and changes only how the waiting is packed onto computers. Cutting what comes in (relevance first, fewer story copies) still helps, but it is the smaller lever, and it stacks on top.

Why packing matters: Trigger.dev bills each computer for every second it is on, and our jobs spend most of those seconds idle, waiting for Gemini to answer. Today every article gets its own computers, so ten articles waiting 36 seconds each cost 360 billed seconds. One computer can hold ten open questions at once, the way one browser can load ten tabs, so the ten waits overlap and we pay for roughly the slowest one.

This does not use Trigger.dev’s pause feature. That feature only switches the computer off while a job waits on another job; here the computer stays on, but one billed computer does ten articles’ waiting instead of one.

The smaller computer

It would cut the price per second in half ($0.0000169 instead of $0.0000338). It only saves money if our jobs run just as fast on it, and we don’t know that yet.

  • Why it might work. The jobs spend most of their time waiting on Gemini and the database, and waiting needs almost no processor or memory. The heavy lifting (searching, scoring text) happens on the database and at Google.
  • What we checked. In a week of runs, no extraction job ran out of memory or crashed on today’s computer (0.5 GB). That only shows 0.5 GB is enough; it says nothing about 0.25 GB. Trigger.dev doesn’t report how much memory a job used.
  • What could go wrong. The small computer has half the processor too. If reading and parsing Gemini’s answers slows down, jobs run longer and eat the saving. It also pulls against packing: ten articles on one computer need more memory than one.
  • How to find out. Move one step, topics, to the small computer for a day, with Trigger.dev’s automatic retry on the normal computer if memory runs out. Compare seconds per run and failures before and after. Update 4: replaced by a side-by-side trial of computer sizes against articles per job (bead B8), because packing may need a bigger computer, not a smaller one.

1. What comes in

Nothing new this round. The copy rule and the source block wait on your answers. The copy rule is new code where articles are saved; the source block is a setting on the feed.

For example, when OpenAI launches a model and 30 sites write it up, “one copy” reads only the first write-up to arrive, while “two copies” also reads the second, so the story survives when the first is a thin rewrite that no room keeps.

2. What we process: relevance check first

  • How it’s built. The manager job asks the relevance check before anything else. The check needs only the article, not the list of people mentioned, so it can go first. If no room wants the article, the manager stops there. Otherwise it passes the answer on to the room-matching step, which today works it out a second time.
  • Three cases must never be skipped. When the check can’t score the article: today it quietly falls back to the old rules, and the new version should do the same. When a curator adds an article to a room by hand. And two older tools that later pull past articles into rooms, without asking the check, would find skipped articles unread.
  • The saving is 13–19% of articles, not a firm 19.5%. The check’s answers are only printed in job logs, never saved. Of last week’s 14,172 articles that reached no room, 9,001 are certain skips and about 4,380 can’t be told apart. The first version should save each answer.
  • Correction: skipping does lose something. We said nobody reads what we extract from articles no room wants. Last week those articles produced 106,524 claims and 16,917 events, and people’s pages (the /m/ pages and profiles) show claims without asking which room they came from. 957 of those claims were by people a room tracks. The loss is small, and it is your call.

3. Seconds per article: several articles per job

  • How it’s built. Each news job already holds its list of new articles but hands them over one at a time, and Trigger.dev cannot merge separate hand-offs into one job. So a new job runs every minute, collects the articles still waiting for a step, and asks all their questions at once.
  • Topics goes first because nothing waits on its answer and running an article twice can’t create duplicates, so a bad trial breaks no page.
  • One piece is missing. Topics leaves no “done” mark, and an article that finds no topics writes nothing, so the collecting job would pick it up again every minute. We add a done mark.
  • Failures stay separate. If one article fails, the job notes it and carries on, and the next round retries it. A job that failed outright would redo all its articles.
  • Still unknown. Each job gets 10 database connections by default, which may cap how many articles are worth packing. The $500–750 stays arithmetic until the trial measures it; a label on each job with its article count lets us read the bill per article.

3. Seconds per article: computer size and articles per job

  • We don’t know yet whether the smaller computer works, and packing pulls the other way. Ten articles open at once hold ten answers in memory, so packing may need a bigger computer, not a smaller one. A bigger computer costs more per second, but if it carries twenty articles it can still be cheaper per article.
  • So we try combinations side by side. Each combination gets its own slice of articles on the same day: today’s setup, the smaller computer with one article, today’s computer with 5 and with 10, and a bigger computer with 10 and with 20. The winner is the lowest cost per article with no more crashes than today.
  • Guardrails so nothing crashes. A job that runs out of memory is rerun on the next size up; there is a ceiling on articles per job and on packed jobs running at once; and each job stays under the 10 database connections it gets. A combination that fails more than today is switched off.
  • How it’s built. Computer size is one setting at hand-off, and articles per job is a setting on the collecting job, so changing a combination needs no new code. The bill shows which computer each job used, because each size has its own price per second.
  • Still unknown. Whether our plan allows the smaller computer; the docs don’t say, and the first deploy will.

3. Seconds per article: Google’s limits

Google doesn’t publish per-minute limits for the model we use; they show only in Google AI Studio for our project. Packing asks the same number of questions, but more of them can land in the same minute.

4. Where results go

No change here. Topics has no reader, which is why it is the safe step for the trial. The one effect on this layer is the correction above: fewer claims on people’s pages.

Beside: market prices

  • How it’s built. Each minute’s 5,436 prices go out in about a dozen writes of 1,000 rows instead of 10,900 single-row writes. Every row stands alone, all share one timestamp, and nothing depends on the order they are written.
  • Also fix. Buy and sell prices are stored the wrong way round on every row, so the gap between them comes out negative. Swapping two lines fixes it.
  • Still unknown. 50 runs failed on 27–29 September while writing a price. The cause is either the reversed prices or the dropped database connections fixed on 30 September. Each batch of 1,000 gets its own error handling, so one bad batch doesn’t stop the rest.

How we’ll know it worked

Each change passes when it does exactly what it set out to do (the right articles skipped, the right step packed) and the bill goes down. Compare cost per article before and after, not the daily total: weekends run about half the volume, so a daily total would mislead.
